Unit 3 · Fuels & energy

Combustion: same atoms, new arrangements

Methane reacts with sufficient oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water. Track one atom all the way to its product.

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g)

Water leaves a flame as vapour. Cooling can condense it; state symbols affect the enthalpy change.

Look for conservation

1 → 1C atoms
4 → 4H atoms
4 → 4O atoms

Coefficients count molecules or moles. Subscripts describe each molecule.

Before revealing: why do we need two O₂ molecules?

Reveal the atom count

CO₂ needs two oxygen atoms, and two H₂O molecules need two more. Four O atoms require 2O₂. The four H atoms in CH₄ form 2H₂O.

Energy is conserved too

Exothermic does not mean “no activation energy”

Schematic profile; the vertical scale is not numerical. The marker follows the teaching timeline, not elapsed reaction time.

Reveal the energy explanation

Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. In this reaction, formation of the product bonds releases more energy than is required to break the reactant bonds. The reacting system loses enthalpy, so ΔH is negative. An ignition source helps overcome the activation-energy barrier.

Guided calculation

How much energy is released?

For this exercise use ΔcH = −890 kJ mol⁻¹ for methane forming liquid water, and M(CH₄) = 16.0 g mol⁻¹. Assume complete combustion.

Reveal the worked calculation

“Energy released” is a positive magnitude. The system’s enthalpy change has a negative sign. These supplied calculation data use H₂O(l), independently of the animation’s water-state selection.

What if oxygen is limited?

Incomplete combustion can form carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot), together with water. Possible balanced equations are:

2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O
CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O

The products can be a mixture; oxygen limitation does not guarantee only one of these pathways. Carbon monoxide is toxic.

Organic preparation · Substitution

Replace a hydrogen, don’t add to carbon

In the source example, ethane forms chloroethane. One H is replaced by Cl and the displaced H appears in hydrogen chloride.

C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl · UV light

Condition: UV light initiates a radical chain by splitting Cl₂. It is not a reagent that contributes atoms to the product.

Focus on what is replaced

2 → 2C atoms
6 → 6H atoms
2 → 2Cl atoms

The carbon skeleton stays intact. Carbon still forms four bonds in chloroethane.

Reveal: why substitution?

One C–H bond is replaced by a C–Cl bond. The displaced hydrogen forms HCl with the other chlorine atom. This is a net replacement, not addition across a multiple bond.

Does excess chlorine stop at one replacement?

No. More H atoms may be replaced and mixtures may form. An excess of the alkane favours monosubstitution relative to further substitution; it does not guarantee a pure product.

Optional mechanism extension · radicals and electron movement

A radical has an unpaired electron (•). UV light starts the chain; propagation regenerates a radical; termination removes radicals. This extension uses methane for clarity.

The two propagation equations add to CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl. Mechanism notation is extension material; follow your teacher’s assessment requirements.

Organic preparation · Haloalkane → alcohol

From chloromethane to methanol

The hydroxide ion supplies the oxygen and hydrogen of the hydroxyl group. Chlorine leaves as chloride.

CH₃Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Cl⁻

Reagent & conditions: aqueous NaOH, with heating in a suitable controlled setup. This is a conceptual reaction, not instructions for bubbling a hazardous gas in a classroom.

Track both atoms and charge

1 → 1O atom
1 → 1Cl atom
−1 → −1Net charge

The three H atoms originally attached to carbon stay attached. The fourth H arrives with OH⁻.

Reveal the full equation

CH₃Cl + NaOH → CH₃OH + NaCl

In solution, Na⁺ is a spectator ion. The net ionic equation makes the reacting hydroxide and departing chloride clearer.

Hydroxide or hydroxyl?

OH⁻ is the hydroxide ion, a reagent. The –OH attached to carbon in an alcohol is the hydroxyl functional group; it is not a free hydroxide ion.

The motion is a simplified bond-change model. It is not a free carbocation being formed and then captured.

Optional mechanism extension · one concerted change

For chloromethane, a lone pair on the hydroxide oxygen forms a bond to carbon as the C–Cl bond breaks. The C–Cl bonding pair goes to chlorine, giving Cl⁻. In an SN2 description these changes occur together through a transition state, not an isolatable five-bonded carbon molecule.

For an electron-pair mechanism, start a full curly arrow at the oxygen lone pair and end it at carbon. A second arrow starts at the C–Cl bond and ends at Cl. The incoming group approaches from the side opposite the leaving group. The animation uses a flat layout for readability, not a 3D geometry model.

Structure check · Explain the source question

Why can’t carbon simply take two more chlorines?

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₄Cl₂ conserves the number of atoms. That alone does not make the proposed single molecule chemically valid.

4 shared pairs = 8 electrons

Each dot represents one valence electron. The diagram shows bonding pairs around carbon; it is a Lewis diagram, not methane’s tetrahedral shape.

Use an electron-dot diagram to explain why CH₄Cl₂ is not the product shown.

Reveal the model answer

In methane, carbon shares four electron pairs with four H atoms. This gives carbon eight electrons in its outer shell. Attaching two additional Cl atoms while retaining all four C–H bonds would require six shared pairs—12 electrons around carbon. Carbon is a period-2 element and does not form this ordinary neutral six-bonded product. A hydrogen must be replaced to retain four bonds: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl.

Reveal the common trap

A balanced atom count is necessary, but does not establish a valid structure or reaction. Do not say “chlorine has no space” or “carbon already has four electrons”: explain four bonding pairs and an octet.

Structure → forces → properties

One –OH group changes the interactions

Distinguish covalent bonds within molecules from the attractions between molecules.

Labelled dashed lines represent intermolecular attractions. They are not covalent bonds and do not represent literal springs. Small movements are illustrative, not a quantitative solubility or boiling simulation.

Is CH₄ less soluble in water than CH₃OH? Explain.

Reveal step 1 · identify polarity and interactions

Water and methanol are polar. Methanol’s oxygen can accept hydrogen bonds from water, and its O–H hydrogen can donate hydrogen bonds to water. Methane is non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.

Reveal step 2 · connect to solubility

Yes. Methane has low solubility in water, whereas methanol mixes with water in all proportions (is miscible). Methanol–water hydrogen bonding helps compensate for attractions disrupted during mixing. Methane cannot offer comparable hydrogen bonding with water. The explanation involves intermolecular interactions, not breaking C–H bonds to dissolve the methane.

Reveal the nuance

All molecules experience dispersion forces, including methanol and water. “Non-polar” does not mean “no forces” or exactly zero solubility. Mixing also depends on entropy; hydrogen bonding is the useful structure-based explanation at this level.

Reveal a connected comparison answer

Ethane is non-polar and has only dispersion attractions between its molecules. Ethanol has a polar –OH group and can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds, as well as dipole–dipole and dispersion attractions. Its stronger overall intermolecular attractions give it a much higher boiling point: ethanol is liquid near room temperature while ethane is gas. Ethanol also hydrogen-bonds with water and is miscible, whereas ethane has low water solubility. Both can undergo complete combustion to form CO₂ and H₂O.

Boiling separates molecules; it does not normally break their covalent C–C, C–H or O–H bonds. Their molar masses also differ, so the boiling-point comparison is not a perfectly isolated test of hydrogen bonding alone.
